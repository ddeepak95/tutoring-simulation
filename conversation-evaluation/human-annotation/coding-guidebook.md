# Coding Guidebook


| Item | Description | Rating Scale | Scale Items / Entry Attributes | Coding Instructions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Instructional Features** |  |  |  |  |
| Concepts | Which main concepts, explanatory units, and explanatory subunits organise the tutor's explanation? | List | `topic` (hierarchy path); concept level (derived from path); `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated); `turn_locations` | **What to record:** Read the whole conversation to identify its main concepts, then visit every tutor section and subsection in source order. Highlight every actual heading, including standalone bold titles, and classify it using the section's meaning. Keep heading-only evidence; inspect the body separately for other instructional features and issues.<br><br>**Hierarchy and concept level:** Use `topic` as a path: **Main concept**, **Main concept :: Explanatory unit**, or **Main concept :: Explanatory unit :: Explanatory subunit**. The **Concept level** attribute is derived from the number of path levels, rather than stored independently. A conversation may have multiple main concepts. Use consistent parent names; Markdown heading depth is a navigation cue rather than a semantic classification. Infer unheaded parents in child paths without inventing highlights.<br><br>**Stable levels:** An explanatory unit is a distinct aspect, method, or purpose under a main concept; a subunit develops that unit through a procedure, result, example, or other specific explanation. For Nucleus identification, Rutherford experiment and Mass spectrometry are units even beneath an Identification methods overview heading. Name generic sections with their local context, such as X-ray diffraction procedure and Electron diffraction procedure; these remain separate. Equivalent original and translated sections should use the same semantic levels when their instructional content is unchanged.<br><br>**Grouping:** Keep one entry per distinct hierarchy node and highlight every heading occurrence for that node. Include introductions, summaries, example sections, and closing invitations under the relevant concept, using descriptive names. Keep parents and descendants as separate entries.<br><br>**Detail levels:** **Brief:** States the main idea with little development. **Explained:** Develops how or why it works, or unpacks an example, comparison, or application. **Elaborated:** Extends the explanation through multiple connected aspects, conditions, comparisons, or applications. Assess detail using the section and its descendant content across relevant turns. These levels describe development; they do not establish correctness. Record issues separately. |
| Examples | Which distinct specific cases or instances does the tutor use to illustrate a learning concept, rule, or principle? | List | `example`; `concept_illustrated`; `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated); `turn_locations` | **What to record:** List each distinct specific case or instance the tutor uses to illustrate a learning concept, rule, or principle. The case may be real or hypothetical. It must be linked to the concept; a general explanation without a specific case is not an example.<br><br>**Entry attributes:** Record `example` (a short description of the specific case), `concept_illustrated` (the concept, rule, or principle it illustrates), `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated), and `turn_locations` (the supporting turns).<br><br>**Coverage audit:** Inspect paragraphs, parenthetical cases, every nested list item, every table data row and example cell, and worked-example subsections. A short named case qualifies as Brief when its instructional connection is supplied by its sentence, section, or table row; an Example heading or standalone explanation is not required. Include table recaps as additional occurrences. For Examples, highlight case-specific data-cell evidence; header-only highlighting applies to Table representations.<br><br>**Distinct entries:** Define an example by its concrete case or scenario and its specific instructional use. Water geometry and water hydrogen bonding are separate uses; H? bond length, scale conversion, and separate method-specific measurement applications are separate scenarios. Merge repeated occurrences only when both case and use match. Keep the connected steps of one calculation or method application together. Separate independent co-listed cases, but keep a deliberate comparison or ordered series together when its teaching point depends on the relationship. Do not double-count a comparison and its members for the same use. Keep one entry for each distinct case/use and highlight every occurrence, including repeated uses in the same turn. Each example highlight has an `example_status` attribute: **Fresh** for the first qualifying occurrence, partial repetitions, and later occurrences that add new example content; **Duplicate** only when the complete example is repeated with no new example content. Compare the whole occurrence, including its setup, facts, worked steps, explanation, and conclusion when present. Reusing the same case, entity, numbers, concept, or selected steps alone does not make an occurrence Duplicate. A shortened reuse of only some elements or an expanded explanation remains Fresh. Different wording or formatting can still be Duplicate if the entire example content is repeated. One case entry may have several Fresh highlights. Supporting fragments of one occurrence share its status. Include all supporting turn locations. Assess its detail level using its development across those turns. Record different cases separately, even when they illustrate the same concept.<br><br>**Detail levels:** **Brief:** Names a specific case and links it to the concept, for example, “Wet clothes drying is an example of evaporation.” **Explained:** Describes how the case illustrates the concept, for example, water in the clothes changes into vapour and enters the surrounding air. **Elaborated:** Develops the case further through reasoning, conditions, comparisons, or worked steps, for example, explaining why clothes dry faster in warm, moving air. These are descriptive detail levels rather than quality scores; a brief example may be sufficient.<br><br>**Worked example:** The tutor says, “Water evaporates from wet clothes as they dry. The water changes from liquid into water vapour and enters the surrounding air.” Record `example`: **Wet clothes drying**; `concept_illustrated`: **Evaporation**; `detail_level`: **Explained**; `turn_locations`: the actual turns containing this example.<br><br>If the tutor also explains water evaporating from a puddle, record **A puddle drying** as a second example, with its concept, detail level, and turn locations. Both illustrate evaporation, but use distinct cases.<br><br>**Explanation without an example:** If the tutor says only, “Evaporation is a change from liquid to gas,” do not record an example: it explains the concept without giving a specific case. |
| Analogies | Which distinct comparisons does the tutor make between a learning concept and another situation or system, using shared features or relationships to explain the concept? | List | `comparison`; `concept_explained`; `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated); `turn_locations` | **What to record:** List each distinct comparison the tutor uses to explain a learning concept through another situation or system. Identify both what is being explained and what it is compared with. The tutor must use a shared feature or relationship to support the explanation; the word “like” alone does not establish an analogy.<br><br>**Entry attributes:** Record `comparison` (a short description naming both sides of the comparison), `concept_explained` (the learning concept the comparison explains), `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated), and `turn_locations` (the supporting turns).<br><br>**Distinct entries:** List repeated use of the same comparison once and include all supporting turn locations. Assess its detail level using its development across those turns. Record comparisons with different situations or systems separately, even when they explain the same concept.<br><br>**Detail levels:** **Brief:** Introduces a comparison and identifies a shared feature or relationship without developing it further. **Explained:** Explains how the features or relationships in one situation correspond to those in the learning concept. **Elaborated:** Develops the comparison through multiple connected correspondences, uses it to reason about the concept, or explains where the comparison stops applying. These are descriptive detail levels rather than quality scores.<br><br>**Worked example:** The tutor says, “Electric current is like water flowing through a pipe. Current describes the rate of flow of electric charge, just as water flow rate describes how much water passes through a pipe each second.” Record `comparison`: **Flow of electric charge compared with water flowing through a pipe**; `concept_explained`: **Electric current**; `detail_level`: **Explained**; `turn_locations`: the actual turns containing this comparison.<br><br>**Example without an analogy:** If the tutor says, “An electric current flows through a lamp when the circuit is closed,” do not record an analogy: the lamp is a specific case of the concept, but no comparison with another situation or system is made. |
| Representations | Which distinct diagrams, graphs, tables, equations, or other visual or symbolic forms does the tutor use to explain or illustrate a learning concept? | List | `representation`; `representation_type` (Diagram / Graph / Table / Equation / Other visual or symbolic form); `concept_illustrated`; `image_languages` (list of BCP 47 language tags); `turn_locations` | **What to record:** List each distinct visual or symbolic representation the tutor provides to explain or illustrate a learning concept. Record representations actually present in the conversation, including images, text-based diagrams, tables, or displayed equations. Do not record a representation that is merely suggested or mentioned without being provided.<br><br>**Entry attributes:** Record `representation` (a short description of what is provided), `representation_type` (Diagram / Graph / Table / Equation / Other visual or symbolic form), `concept_illustrated` (the concept it helps explain), `image_languages` (a list of languages visibly written inside the representation, using BCP 47 tags such as `en`, `ta`, or `hi`), and `turn_locations` (where it appears). Record every visible written language: for example, use `["ta", "en"]` for Tamil and English. Use `["zxx"]` for no written language and `["und"]` when text is present but its language cannot be reliably identified. For Other, specify the form.<br><br>**Distinct entries:** List repeated use of the same representation once and include all supporting turn locations. Record distinct representations separately, even when they illustrate the same concept.<br><br>**Worked example:** The tutor provides a temperature–time graph showing a heating curve and explains the flat section during a phase change. Record `representation`: **Temperature–time graph showing a heating curve**; `representation_type`: **Graph**; `concept_illustrated`: **Heating and phase changes**; `image_languages`: **["en"]**; `turn_locations`: the actual turns where the graph appears.<br><br>**Mention without a representation:** If the tutor only says, “You could draw a graph of temperature against time,” but provides no graph, do not list a representation. |
| Cultural References | Which distinct references to cultural practices, traditions, artifacts, stories, media, or shared cultural experiences does the tutor use to explain or illustrate a learning concept? | List | `reference`; `concept_illustrated`; `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated); `turn_locations` | **What to record:** List each distinct reference to a cultural practice, tradition, or artifact that the tutor uses to explain or illustrate a learning concept. The cultural connection and its instructional use must be evident; exclude incidental cultural mentions.<br><br>**Entry attributes:** Record `reference` (the specific cultural reference used), `concept_illustrated` (the learning concept it helps explain), `detail_level` (Brief / Explained / Elaborated), and `turn_locations` (the supporting turns).<br><br>**Distinct entries:** List repeated use of the same reference once and include all supporting turn locations. Assess its detail level using its development across those turns.<br><br>**Detail levels:** **Brief:** Names the cultural reference and links it to the concept. **Explained:** Explains how the reference illustrates the concept. **Elaborated:** Develops the connection through reasoning, comparisons, or applications. These are descriptive detail levels rather than quality scores.<br><br>**Worked example:** The tutor discusses fireworks used during a named cultural festival and explains how heating atoms leads them to emit characteristic colours. Record `reference`: **Fireworks during the named festival** (using the festival actually named); `concept_illustrated`: **Atomic emission**; `detail_level`: **Explained**; `turn_locations`: the actual turns containing this reference and explanation.<br><br>**Everyday example without an explicit cultural connection:** An explanation of fireworks alone is an everyday example; list it as a cultural reference only when the tutor explicitly connects it to a cultural celebration or practice.<br><br>**Incidental cultural mention:** A festival greeting without using the festival to explain a learning concept is not a qualifying cultural reference.<br><br>**Cultural artifacts:** References to objects such as traditional textiles, artworks, or musical instruments also qualify when the tutor uses their cultural context or features to explain a learning concept. For example, using repeating motifs in a traditional textile to explain symmetry can qualify; merely naming the textile does not. |
| Questions | Which questions does the tutor direct to the learner to elicit a response? | List | `question`; `question_type` (Knowledge-checking / Instructional); `concept_checked` (knowledge being checked, or null); `turn_locations` | **What to record:** Record only actual questions directed to the learner with an expectation of a learner response. The learner need not actually answer for the question to qualify. Record each eligible question occurrence separately, including repetitions. Exclude learner-only questions and questions used as section headings.<br><br>**Eligibility before classification:** Read the surrounding tutor content before assigning a question type. Exclude rhetorical questions that the tutor answers itself as part of its explanation, including a question followed by its definition, calculation, reasoning, or conclusion without inviting the learner to respond. Subject-matter wording or a question mark alone does not establish eligibility. A genuine learner-directed knowledge check remains eligible even if a later tutor turn explains the answer after a learner response or opportunity to respond.<br><br>**Closing invitations and suggested doubts:** Exclude statements or imperatives inviting the learner to ask questions, such as "If you have any other doubt, ask!", "We can explore in more detail", or "Let me know if you have questions." These are not questions to the learner. Also exclude lists of suggested questions the learner could ask the tutor, unless the tutor instead asks the learner to answer them. An actual learner-directed question such as "Do you have any questions?" or "Would you like another example?" remains eligible as Instructional. Do not convert an invitation into a question or include the surrounding invitation text in the highlighted question.<br><br>**Entry attributes:** Record `question` (the exact eligible question wording, preserving source language, Markdown, and punctuation), `question_type` (Knowledge-checking / Instructional), `concept_checked` (the knowledge or concept being checked for Knowledge-checking questions; null for Instructional questions), and `turn_locations` (the single tutor turn containing that occurrence). Record separate questions in the same turn as separate entries.<br><br>**Knowledge-checking:** The tutor asks the learner to recall, explain, compare, reason about, or apply subject-matter knowledge. It requests a demonstration of knowledge rather than a self-report of understanding. Classify it this way even if the learner does not answer, provided the question is genuinely posed to the learner rather than self-answered rhetorically.<br><br>**Instructional:** An eligible question manages the learning interaction without requesting a demonstration of subject-matter knowledge. This includes self-report checks such as "Have you understood?" or "Does that make sense?", actual questions such as "Do you have any questions?", and preference questions such as "Would you like another example?" Rhetorical self-answered questions, suggested learner doubts, and statements inviting questions do not qualify under either question type.<br><br>**Worked example - Knowledge-checking:** The tutor asks, "Why do wet clothes dry faster in moving air?", inviting the learner to explain the effect of airflow. Record that exact question as Knowledge-checking, with `concept_checked`: **Effect of airflow on evaporation**.<br><br>**Worked example - Instructional understanding check:** The tutor asks, "Have you understood?" Record that exact question as Instructional, with `concept_checked`: **null**.<br><br>**Excluded example - Rhetorical:** "What is evaporation? Evaporation is the change of liquid water into vapour." The tutor asks and answers its own explanatory question. Do not create a Questions annotation; if the question is a heading, retain its Concepts hierarchy annotation.<br><br>**Excluded example - Closing invitation:** "Or, if you have any other doubt, ask! We can explore in more detail. ??" Do not create a Questions annotation: this is an invitation, not a question requesting a learner response. |
| **Pedagogical Characteristics** |  |  |  |  |
| Instructional Sequence | Does the tutor explain the core learning concept before presenting an example, or introduce an example before explaining the concept? | Nominal categorical | Rule → Example: Explains the core concept or principle first, then illustrates it with an example.; Example → Rule: Presents an example first, then uses it to introduce or explain the core concept or principle.; Not determinable: The conversation lacks either an explanation or an example, or begins after the concept has already been introduced. | Identify the core learning concept from the task or initial learner request. Assess how the tutor first introduces that concept, excluding sequences used for supporting concepts or later follow-ups.<br><br>**Rule → Example:** The tutor first states a general definition, principle, or explanation of the core concept, then illustrates it with a specific case.<br><br>**Example → Rule:** The tutor first presents a specific case, then uses it to introduce or explain the general concept.<br><br>**Not determinable:** Either component is absent, their order is unclear, or the conversation begins after the concept has already been introduced.<br><br>Naming the topic alone does not count as explaining the rule.<br><br>**Worked example — Rule → Example:** “Evaporation is liquid changing into gas at its surface. Wet clothes drying illustrates this process.”<br><br>**Worked example — Example → Rule:** “Think about wet clothes drying: their water enters the air as vapour. This change from liquid to gas at the surface is called evaporation.”<br><br>Neither sequence receives a higher score; this item describes instructional organization. |
| Concept Summarization | Does the tutor recap the key points of each newly explained concept? | Ordinal | 3 = Recaps the key points after every new concept explanation.; 2 = Summarizes some, but not all, newly explained concepts; 1 = Never | Identify each newly explained concept and check whether the tutor provides a brief recap of its key points after the explanation. A summary condenses ideas already explained; introducing new information or repeating an isolated sentence does not qualify.<br><br>**3:** Summarizes every newly explained concept. **2:** Summarizes some, but not all, newly explained concepts. **1:** Does not summarize any newly explained concept.<br><br>Do not require a summary after every turn. A combined recap can cover multiple concepts if it clearly summarizes the key points of each.<br><br>**Worked example:** After explaining evaporation and its influencing factors, the tutor says, “To recap, evaporation happens at a liquid’s surface, and higher temperature, greater exposed surface area, and moving air increase its rate.” This qualifies because it condenses the preceding explanation into its main points.<br><br>**Transition without a summary:** “Now let’s move on to boiling” does not qualify: it marks a transition without recapping the explanation.<br><br>**Not applicable:** If no new concept is explained, record Not applicable rather than assigning 1. |
| Coherence | Do the tutor’s explanations and examples build on one another in a logical sequence across turns? | Ordinal | 3 = Explanations and examples consistently connect to preceding content, forming a logical sequence across turns.; 2 = Some explanations and examples connect to preceding content, but others contain abrupt transitions or unexplained gaps.; 1 = Explanations and examples are largely disconnected, with little logical progression across turns. | Assess whether the tutor’s explanations and examples form a logical progression across turns. Look for connections that show how new content builds on preceding ideas. These may be explicit or clear from the explanation; words such as “because” or “therefore” alone do not establish coherence.<br><br>**3:** Connections are consistently clear, with no substantive unexplained gaps. **2:** Some connections are clear, but other transitions leave the learner to infer a missing link. **1:** Explanations are largely disconnected, with little logical progression.<br><br>A change of topic is not automatically incoherent if the tutor explains the connection. Assess logical connections separately from factual correctness and relevance to the learning objective.<br><br>**Worked example:** The tutor first explains evaporation as particles escaping a liquid’s surface. In a later turn, it says, “Higher temperature gives more particles enough energy to escape, so evaporation becomes faster.” This clearly connects a new explanation to the preceding concept.<br><br>**Unexplained gap:** If the tutor instead moves from evaporation to “Temperature affects density” without explaining the relationship or transition, that is evidence of a gap. One gap supports **2**, rather than automatically making the whole conversation **1**. |
| Information Load Appropriateness | Across the conversation, does the tutor provide sufficient explanation and introduce information in manageable portions, considering the learner’s questions and any expressed needs? | Ordinal | 3 = Provides the essential details needed to follow the explanations, introduces new information in manageable portions, and avoids unnecessary detail or repetition. Adjusts the amount of information when the learner expresses a need.; 2 = Generally provides sufficient, manageable explanations, but some parts omit essential details, introduce several ideas without breaking them down, or include unnecessary detail or repetition. Responds inconsistently to expressed needs for more or less information.; 1 = Frequently omits essential details or presents information without breaking it into manageable portions. Substantial unnecessary detail or repetition occurs, or expressed needs for more or less information repeatedly go unaddressed. | Assess the amount and organization of information across the conversation. Judge whether explanations provide the details needed to follow the concept without unnecessary detail, repetition, or too many new ideas introduced at once.<br><br>**3:** Essential details are provided, new information is broken into manageable parts, and the tutor adjusts when the learner requests more detail or simplification. **2:** Explanations are generally sufficient and manageable, but some parts omit needed details, introduce several ideas without breaking them down, or contain unnecessary detail or repetition. **1:** These problems recur or substantially affect the central explanation, or requests for more or less information repeatedly go unaddressed.<br><br>Use the learner’s questions and expressed needs as evidence. Do not infer overload from length alone or assume confusion means excessive information. Assess adjustment only when an opportunity arises.<br><br>**Worked example:** The learner asks why clothes dry faster in moving air. The tutor explains that moving air removes water vapour near the clothes, allowing evaporation to continue faster, and adds detail when requested. This supports an appropriate information load.<br><br>**Insufficient detail:** An answer such as “Because of evaporation,” without explaining airflow’s role, does not provide the details needed to answer the learner’s question.<br><br>**Too much information at once:** A lengthy explanation introducing vapour pressure, humidity, diffusion, and thermodynamics without developing their connections may provide too much information at once. Assess how the ideas are presented, rather than length alone.<br><br>**Distinguishing 2 from 1:** Assign **2** for occasional, localized problems within otherwise manageable explanations. Assign **1** when problems recur or substantially affect the central explanation. A single problem may justify **1** if it leaves the main reasoning of the central explanation difficult to follow. |
| Relevance | Do the tutor’s explanations, examples, and responses to follow-up questions support the core learning objective or address learner questions related to it? | Ordinal | 3 = Explanations, examples, and follow-up responses consistently support the core learning objective. Additional topics serve a clear purpose, such as explaining a prerequisite or answering a related learner question.; 2 = Content generally supports the core learning objective, but some explanations, examples, or follow-up responses introduce tangents without a clear contribution to it.; 1 = Content frequently shifts to topics that do not support the core learning objective, leaving the intended topic or related learner questions insufficiently addressed. | Identify the core learning objective from the task or initial learner request. Assess whether the tutor’s explanations, examples, and follow-up responses contribute to that objective.<br><br>Additional topics can be relevant when they explain a prerequisite, clarify the concept, or answer a related learner question. Introducing another topic does not automatically constitute a tangent.<br><br>**3:** Content consistently contributes to the learning objective. **2:** Content generally contributes, but some passages introduce tangents without a clear purpose. **1:** Content frequently shifts away from the objective, leaving the intended topic or related learner questions insufficiently addressed.<br><br>Judge what the content contributes, rather than how smoothly it connects to preceding content. Assess real-world usefulness separately.<br><br>**Worked example:** The learner asks how temperature affects evaporation. The tutor explains particle energy before connecting it to the rate of evaporation. The particle explanation is relevant because it supports the requested explanation.<br><br>**Tangent:** If the tutor instead describes temperature scales and their history without using that information to explain evaporation, that passage is a tangent. A localized tangent within an otherwise relevant conversation supports **2**; repeated shifts that leave the learning objective insufficiently addressed support **1**. |
| Real-World Connection and Usefulness | Does the tutor explicitly explain how the learning topic relates to real-world situations or serves a useful purpose? | Ordinal | 3 = Clearly explains how the topic helps interpret a concrete real-world situation or supports a practical use.; 2 = Mentions a real-world situation or practical use, but leaves its connection to the topic partly unexplained.; 1 = Provides no real-world connection or useful purpose, or only makes a vague claim such as “This is important.” | Look for an explicit connection between the learning topic and an everyday situation or practical purpose.<br><br>**3:** Explains how the concept helps understand the situation or accomplish a practical goal. **2:** Names a related situation or use but leaves the connection partly unexplained. **1:** Provides no connection or useful purpose, or only claims that the topic is important.<br><br>Personalization is not required. A familiar object alone does not establish a meaningful connection.<br><br>**Worked example — 3:** “Spreading wet clothes out exposes more surface area, increasing evaporation and helping them dry faster.” This connects the concept to a practical action and explains why it works.<br><br>**Worked example — 2:** “Evaporation happens when clothes dry,” without further explanation, names a real-world situation but leaves the connection partly unexplained. |
| Appropriateness for the Learner’s Level | Do the tutor’s vocabulary, conceptual complexity, mathematical detail, and assumed prior knowledge match the learner’s stated level and demonstrated understanding? | Ordinal | 3 = Consistently uses language, conceptual complexity, and mathematical detail suited to the learner. Explains unfamiliar terms, notation, and prerequisite steps when needed, and adjusts to demonstrated understanding.; 2 = Generally matches the learner’s level, but some explanations use unexplained terminology or notation, assume unsupported knowledge, omit needed mathematical steps, or are unnecessarily basic. Adjustments are inconsistent.; 1 = Frequently uses language, concepts, or mathematical detail that is too advanced or too basic for the learner, with little adjustment to clear indications of their understanding. | Use the stated learner level, supplied profile, and understanding demonstrated through the learner’s contributions. Assess whether vocabulary, conceptual complexity, mathematical detail, and assumed prior knowledge fit that evidence.<br><br>**3:** Explanations consistently fit the learner’s level. Unfamiliar terms, notation, and prerequisite steps are explained when needed. **2:** Explanations generally fit, but some passages assume unsupported knowledge, omit necessary steps, or are unnecessarily basic. **1:** Explanations frequently mismatch the learner’s level, with little adjustment to clear evidence of their understanding.<br><br>Assess mathematical detail only where relevant. Do not infer ability from age alone, and assess adjustment only when an opportunity arises. If learner-level evidence is insufficient, record that limitation.<br><br>**Worked example:** The learner says, “I haven’t learned about particles yet.” The tutor introduces particles in plain language before using them to explain evaporation. This supports alignment with the learner’s level.<br><br>**Mismatch:** Using unexplained terms such as “molecular kinetic-energy distribution” despite that statement provides evidence of a mismatch. An occasional mismatch supports **2**; repeated mismatches support **1**.<br><br>Judge the difficulty and assumed knowledge here; assess the amount of information under Information Load Appropriateness. |
| Opportunities to Demonstrate Understanding | Does the tutor provide an opportunity for the learner to practise or demonstrate understanding of the core learning concept, during the conversation or through a follow-up task? | Ordinal | 3 = Provides a concrete question or task requiring the learner to explain or apply the core learning concept, during the conversation or as follow-up practice.; 2 = Provides only a recall question, a general self-report check, or a vague suggestion for related practice.; 1 = Provides no opportunity to demonstrate understanding or practise the core learning concept. | Identify the core learning concept from the task or initial learner request. Assess opportunities to practise or demonstrate that concept, rather than requiring a check after every supporting concept. Either a prompt during the conversation or a follow-up practice task can qualify.<br><br>**3:** A concrete question or task requires explanation or application of the core learning concept. **2:** Opportunities are limited to recall, general self-report checks, or vague suggestions for related practice. **1:** No opportunity is provided to demonstrate understanding or practise the core learning concept.<br><br>A score of **3** indicates that an opportunity was provided, not that the learner demonstrated understanding or completed the task. Assess the tutor’s prompt even if the learner does not respond. Multiple questions or tasks are not required. |
| **Conversation Quality** |  |  |  |  |
| Conversational Naturalness | Does the tutor use familiar conversational phrases and address the learner personally, making the exchange feel like a dialogue rather than simply delivering explanations? | Ordinal | 3 = Consistently uses familiar conversational phrases and personal wording tied to the learner’s contributions, making explanations feel part of a dialogue.; 2 = Uses some conversational phrases and personal wording, but these are often generic or repetitive, and parts of the exchange feel like standalone explanations.; 1 = Primarily delivers impersonal explanations, with little conversational phrasing or personal acknowledgement of the learner’s contributions. | Assess whether the tutor’s wording feels like dialogue with this learner, using familiar conversational phrases and personal references that fit the exchange.<br><br>**3:** Conversational phrasing and personal wording consistently fit the learner’s contributions. **2:** Some wording feels conversational and personal, but other parts use generic, repetitive phrases or read like standalone explanations. **1:** Responses predominantly deliver impersonal explanations with little conversational phrasing or personal acknowledgement.<br><br>**Worked example:** The learner says, “I’ve noticed clothes dry faster near our fan.” The tutor replies, “That’s a useful observation—let’s use your fan example to explore what moving air does.” This provides evidence of conversational, personal wording.<br><br>Repeatedly starting unrelated responses with “Great question!” provides weaker evidence because the phrase does not reflect the particular contribution.<br><br>Judge the pattern across the conversation; one natural phrase alone does not establish **3**. |
| Responsiveness | Does the tutor address the learner’s questions, requests, and expressed confusion, and adapt explanations to demonstrated prior knowledge, misconceptions, or stated interests when relevant? | Ordinal | 3 = Consistently addresses the learner’s contributions and makes relevant adaptations—for example, building on demonstrated knowledge, correcting a misconception, or using an expressed interest to shape an example.; 2 = Addresses some learner contributions, but responses are sometimes incomplete or generic, or miss clear opportunities to adapt the explanation.; 1 = Frequently ignores or misinterprets learner contributions, or continues generic explanations despite clear indications that adaptation is needed. | Compare the tutor’s responses with the learner’s questions, requests, expressed confusion, and other relevant contributions. Look for direct answers and adaptations based on demonstrated knowledge, misconceptions, or stated interests.<br><br>**3:** Consistently addresses the points raised and adapts explanations when relevant opportunities arise. **2:** Addresses some contributions, but others receive incomplete or generic responses, or clear opportunities for adaptation are missed. **1:** Frequently ignores or misinterprets contributions, or continues generic explanations despite clear indications that adaptation is needed.<br><br>Assess adaptation only when supported by the learner’s contributions or supplied profile. When no adaptation opportunity arises, assess how directly the tutor addresses the learner’s contributions.<br><br>**Worked example:** The learner says, “I thought evaporation happens only when water boils.” The tutor replies, “Evaporation can happen below boiling temperature. It occurs at the surface, while boiling occurs throughout the liquid.” This directly addresses the misconception and adapts the explanation.<br><br>**Missed contribution:** A reply such as “Evaporation is important in the water cycle,” without addressing the misconception, provides evidence of poor responsiveness.<br><br>Judge the pattern across the conversation; one missed contribution does not automatically establish **1**. |
| Positivity of Tone | How much warmth, encouragement, or empathy does the tutor express toward the learner through its wording and responses? | Ordinal | 3 = High positivity: Consistently conveys warmth, encouragement, or empathy through wording suited to the learner’s contributions.; 2 = Moderate positivity: Uses some warm, encouraging, or empathetic wording, but the tone is predominantly matter-of-fact.; 1 = Low positivity: Shows little or no warmth, encouragement, or empathy; the tone is neutral or negative. | Assess the warmth, encouragement, and empathy expressed in the tutor’s wording across the conversation.<br><br>**3:** Consistently conveys positive tone suited to the learner’s contributions. **2:** Uses some warm, encouraging, or empathetic wording, but the tone is predominantly matter-of-fact. **1:** Shows little or no positive tone; responses are neutral or negative.<br><br>Judge the overall pattern rather than an isolated phrase. Record dismissive or discouraging wording when present.<br><br>**Worked example — High positivity:** “You’ve identified the key factor. This distinction can be tricky—let’s work through it together.”<br><br>**Worked example — Moderate positivity:** Mostly factual explanations, with occasional encouragement such as “That’s a useful observation.”<br><br>**Worked example — Low positivity:** Consistently matter-of-fact explanations without warmth or encouragement, or wording such as “You should already know this.”<br><br>A neutral tone can receive **1** without being disrespectful; this item measures positivity, not overall teaching quality. |
